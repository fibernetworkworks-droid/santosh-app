# Timing, not the voice, makes calls feel human

A live AI call feels human mostly because of **when** it speaks, not how pretty its voice is. People answer each other in about a fifth of a second, and they start to hear a pause as doubt or unwillingness at roughly 600-800 thousandths of a second (milliseconds, written "ms" below). A voice that answers on time, stops at once when you cut in, does not jump in during your thinking pauses, and keeps one steady voice will feel more human than a beautiful voice with bad timing. On single sentences, computer voices already score as well as real recordings; what still gives them away is fitting the flow of a real conversation ([Sesame research](https://sesame.com/research)). Across the world's languages, the picture is uneven: speech recognition now reaches more than 1,600 languages on paper, but quality falls sharply for languages with under about 10 hours of recorded training speech, and live voice products cover far fewer, roughly 24 to 99 depending on which Google page you read ([arXiv 2511.09690](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09690); [Gemini Live capabilities](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/capabilities)). For Indian languages and Hinglish, Gemini Live is still the widest single choice, and no outside test yet proves any rival sounds better in Hinglish. For Hoola, whose Gemini call works and must not break, the right move is to **measure first and change almost nothing**: keep Gemini Live as the voice, keep the Haiku brain behind it, keep one voice, and only try small, switchable, tested changes on a copy of the call before any real user hears them.

## Humans answer in about 200 ms, and a long pause sounds like "no"

The clearest finding in the research is about timing. A 2009 study filmed conversations in 10 languages on five continents and found that every language avoids talking over each other and keeps the silence between turns short; each language's average sits within about a quarter of a second of the overall average ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19553212/)). Japanese was fastest at about 7 ms and Danish slowest at about 469 ms ([altalang summary](https://altalang.com/beyond-words/taking-turns-in-conversation/)). A later study measured **199 ms in Dutch and 242 ms in English** ([doi 10.3390/languages8020115](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages8020115)). Since it takes a person over 600 ms to plan a sentence, people must start planning their reply while the other person is still talking, guessing where the sentence will end ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731/full)).

Silence also carries meaning. In simulated phone calls, raising the silence before a reply from 0 to 600 to 1,200 ms made listeners judge the speaker as less willing each time, and silence length mattered more than tone of voice ([EMCA wiki](https://emcawiki.net/Roberts2006)). English, Italian and Japanese listeners all reacted the same way ([EMCA wiki](https://emcawiki.net/Roberts2011)), and a German study saw willingness drop most above about 600 ms ([SDU](https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/how-long-is-too-long-how-pause-features-after-requests-affect-the/)). The exact tipping point is not settled, but it is clearly well under one second.

Today's AI calls are slower than that. The usual "relay" design, where one program turns speech into text, a second writes a reply, and a third reads it aloud, takes about **1,000-1,500 ms a turn** ([arXiv 2404.16053](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2404.16053)). Simply waiting for a fixed stretch of silence does not fix it: a 500 ms silence rule caught only about half the real turn endings in two recorded sets of conversations, and also wrongly cut in during pauses inside one person's turn ([Heldner & Edlund](https://staff.fnwi.uva.nl/r.fernandezrovira/teaching/cosp/cosp2016/docs/HeldnerEdlund2010.pdf)). A good call has to judge the end of a turn from meaning and tone, such as a finished sentence or a falling voice, not from silence alone. Builders also find that **steady speed beats a fast average with spikes**: a reliable 900 ms feels better than 700 ms on average with occasional 2.5-second stalls ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/learn/voice-ai-latency-why-pauses-read-as-hesitation)). A sensible target, which is this report's own reading of the studies rather than an official standard, is the first sound within about 500 ms half the time and within 800 ms nineteen times out of twenty.

The small sounds of listening matter too, but only when timed well. "Hmm", "haan" and "accha" at the right moments made a voice seem more human, while the same sounds at random moments, or too many or too few, made it worse ([University of Twente](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/backchannels-quantity-type-and-timing-matters/)). Fillers like "um" made a computer voice sound more real and less sure of itself, without making it sound less natural ([KTH](https://www.speech.kth.se/stance/papers/kirkland22_interspeech.pdf); [KTH podcast voice](https://www.speech.kth.se/tts-demos/is19/szekely2019spontaneous.pdf)). That cuts both ways: a filler is useful to cover a short wait ("hmm, ek second") but wrong in a confident answer. Copying the caller also needs a light touch. Matching the caller's loudness raised trust, but matching their pitch lowered it, and too much copying felt bad ([Columbia](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/speech/PaperFiles/2020/Galvez_speech_communication_2020.pdf)). No one has yet studied listening sounds or fillers in Indian languages, and no proper study has measured fake breaths or laughter on live calls.

Second, **no independent test anywhere compares these models for Hindi or Hinglish naturalness**, so "best for Hinglish" can only be settled by Hoola's own listening tests.

Google has also taken some dials away. On Gemini 3.8 Live, the "emotional reply" switch has been removed and must not be sent, and "proactive audio" (the model deciding by itself when to speak up) is always on; turning it off returns an error ([Gemini Live capabilities](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/capabilities)). So on 3.8 the levers left are the choice among about 30 ready-made voices, the written persona, the setting for how long a silence ends her turn (Google suggests 500-800 ms, default about 800), and the plumbing that keeps a long call alive ([capabilities](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/capabilities); [speech generation](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/speech-generation)).

## One model that listens while it talks beats a relay, but not on everything

There are two ways to build a live AI call. In a **relay** ("cascaded") design, separate programs hear, think and speak in turn. In a **listen-while-talking** ("full-duplex") design, one model hears and speaks at the same time, the way a person does on the phone. The duplex style wins on feel: it can answer in about 200 ms, let you interrupt naturally and keep the tone of voice from start to finish; Kyutai's open Moshi model reached about 200 ms in practice ([arXiv 2410.00037](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.00037v1)). The relay style wins on control: you can pick any brain and any voice, read the text, check tool steps and price each stage, and it reaches more languages, which is why India's Sarvam is built as a relay ([Sarvam docs](https://docs.sarvam.ai/voice-agents/build/models.md)).

Tests from 2025 show that no system was good at both speed and manners. Moshi answered in 0.265 seconds but jumped in during **98.5% of the caller's pauses**; Gemini Live jumped in during only 25.5% of pauses but took 1.3 seconds to answer, and scored 3.4 out of 5 for its reply after being interrupted, against Moshi's 0.8 ([Full-Duplex-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04721v3)). Every system tested also timed its "hmm" sounds poorly compared with humans ([Full-Duplex-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04721v3)). Newer tests add the noise of real homes, such as someone else talking in the room, which matters a lot for Indian households ([Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23159v2)).

The industry is now meeting in the middle: a duplex voice in front, a separate brain behind. OpenAI's GPT-Live-1 is built exactly this way ([cellcog](https://cellcog.ai/blog/gpt-live-1/)), and so is Hoola's own call, where Gemini speaks and a Claude Haiku brain does the work through one tool ([Hoola CLAUDE.md](/home/user/santosh-app/CLAUDE.md)). The one thing a duplex model cannot give is a specific outside voice, such as a Sarvam voice or a cloned one. Getting that means moving to a relay and accepting worse timing and an extra delay at every step.

A lot of the "robot" feeling is not the model at all but the sound path on the phone. Echo, where the AI hears its own voice through the speaker and thinks you interrupted, and audio that keeps playing after you start talking, are the most common giveaways. The WebRTC calling technology used in browsers removes echo and smooths bad networks by itself, but a plain data stream like a WebSocket does not, so the app must handle echo itself ([Pipecat](https://docs.pipecat.ai/client/concepts/choosing-a-transport)). Google's own advice is to send sound in small 20-40 ms pieces, never hold back a second of it, and **wipe the playback the instant the model reports an interruption** ([Gemini Live best practices](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/best-practices)). Hoola already learned one of these lessons the hard way: pre-recorded "ek second" clips from a different voice engine made calls sound like "radio, different voices", so they were switched off and only Gemini's own voice speaks now ([Hoola CLAUDE.md](/home/user/santosh-app/CLAUDE.md)).

## "Every language" breaks down below 10 hours of recordings

Coverage numbers sound huge but hide a steep drop. Meta's Omnilingual speech recognition, released in November 2025, covers more than 1,600 languages, 500 of them never covered before ([arXiv 2511.09690](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09690)). By Meta's own reported figures, 95% of languages with at least 10 hours of training speech reach an error rate under 10% of characters, but **only 36% of languages with less than 10 hours do** ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/metas-omnilingual-asr-brings-speech-recognition-to-1600-languages/)). Speaking voices lag further behind hearing. Meta's older system that speaks about 1,100 languages was trained mostly on Bible readings with few speakers per language ([Meta AI](https://ai.meta.com/blog/multilingual-model-speech-recognition/)), and some of its languages "sound expressive and human, while others remain robotic or unintelligible" ([Turing](https://www.turing.com/resources/multilingual-tts-systems)). Understandable is not the same as natural.

New languages are being added the same way everywhere: lots of everyday crowd recordings to teach hearing, plus a small set of clean studio recordings to teach speaking. Google's WAXAL project for Sub-Saharan Africa grew to 27 languages with about 1,846 hours for hearing and 565 studio hours for speaking by March 2026 ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/waxal-a-large-scale-open-resource-for-african-language-speech-technology/)), and Mozilla's Common Voice now covers 294 languages ([Common Voice](https://github.com/common-voice/cv-dataset)). Commercial African products are still small; Lelapa AI hears four or five South African languages and its speaking voice is "coming soon" ([Lelapa docs](https://docs.lelapa.ai/overview/introduction)).

Small languages fail in recognisable ways. Models answer in the **wrong script or language**: on some South Asian languages this pushed OpenAI Whisper's error rate above 100%, and simply converting the output to the right script won back up to 25 points ([Interspeech 2026](https://aghaaliraza.com/publications/2026--Interspeech--Dissecting_ASR_Failures_in_Low-Resource_South_Asian_Languages.pdf)). Models invent or swap words in very small languages ([Sunbird AI](https://salt.sunbird.ai/models/asr-whisper-51-african-languages/)). In tonal languages, where pitch changes the meaning of a word, voices can sound natural yet get the tones wrong; all four systems tested for the Nigerian language Efik struggled with this ([Modelwire](https://themodelwire.com/article/first-efik-text-to-speech-study-exposes-tonal-synthesis-gaps-in-low-resource-ai-01KWXA8BJYJX9K0BNKCAF8QH3M)). Numbers are a trap in every language, because one written figure has many correct spoken forms; India's Sarvam tests on cases like "₹8,750" and "15 जनवरी" for exactly this reason ([Sarvam](https://sarvam.ai/blogs/bulbul-v3)).

India has the richest set of tools outside English and Chinese. AI4Bharat's open voices cover 11 to 21 languages under permissive licences ([IndicF5](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/IndicF5); [Indic Parler-TTS](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/indic-parler-tts)), and Sarvam's Bulbul voice won a blind test of 20,000 votes on phone-quality sound, though that test was run for Sarvam and reported by Sarvam ([Sarvam](https://sarvam.ai/blogs/bulbul-v3)). Gemini Live lists Odia, Assamese and Sindhi, which makes it likely the only live option for those, but nobody has measured how good it is in them.

## Hinglish and accents: the voice must switch with the phrase

Mixed speech like Hinglish is where tools differ most. On the same Hinglish recordings, the error rate of different hearing models ranged from **about 27% to about 70%**, according to a benchmark reported by Deepgram, itself a vendor ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/blog/hinglish-voice-ai-speech-recognition)). Models trained on one language at a time "struggle with code-switched input" ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12329218/)). That spread means the choice of model matters far more for Hinglish than for plain Hindi. Most open Indian speaking voices say nothing about mixed or Roman-script input ([Sonex Labs](https://www.sonexlabs.com/blog/best-open-source-hindi-tts)), so in a relay design Hoola would need an extra step to decide that "kal milte hain" is Hindi and not English spelled out. A speak-and-listen model trained on real mixed speech, like Gemini, avoids that step.

Accents raise a separate problem called "accent leakage": a cloned voice carries the accent of the language it was recorded in. Clone a Hindi speaker into Tamil and, by default, it sounds like a Hindi speaker speaking Tamil; this happens even in a model that covers more than 600 languages, and it gets worse when one sentence mixes languages ([arXiv 2609.01016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.01016); [arXiv 2606.07240](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.07240)). The fixes in 2026 research are still mostly unproven preprints. In practice, the way to sound native is a native speaker's voice per language. OpenAI's advice is useful here: tell the model "do not infer language from accent alone" and to ignore short sounds like "haan" or a single foreign word when deciding what language she is speaking ([OpenAI voice prompting](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-prompting)). No study measures how well these systems handle regional Indian accents, such as Bhojpuri-flavoured Hindi.

## Cloning a voice needs a live yes, and the AI must say it is an AI

The law has caught up in 2026. In the **European Union, since 2 August 2026**, an AI that talks to people must tell them it is an AI at the first moment of contact, in a way they actually notice, and an AI acting for someone must also say for whom; fines reach €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover ([Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content)). In **India, since 20 February 2026**, amended IT Rules require AI-made audio that seems real to carry a "prominently prefixed audio disclosure", meaning a spoken notice at the start ([Khaitan & Co](https://www.khaitanco.com/thought-leadership/MeitY-notifies-the-IT-Amendment-Rules-2026)). Whether this covers a private one-to-one call, rather than published content, is not settled. India's data protection rules add clear, purpose-specific consent duties from about May 2027 ([PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf)). In the **United States**, an AI voice counts as an "artificial voice" under phone-call law, so AI calls need prior consent ([FCC](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-makes-ai-generated-voices-robocalls-illegal)), and Tennessee's ELVIS Act makes copying someone's voice without consent unlawful, including for the makers of tools built to do it ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/04/tennessee-law-addresses-proliferation-of-deepfakes)).

Good practice on cloning is stricter than most law. ElevenLabs lets people make a high-quality clone only of their own voice, "even with their consent, you cannot clone someone else's voice", and checks it by having the speaker read a set statement live ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/product/voices/voice-cloning/can-i-create-a-professional-voice-clone-of-someone-elses-voice.md)). Hidden marks in the sound, such as Google's SynthID, let a clip later be identified as AI-made ([Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/technologies/synthid/)), though no Google page confirmed that Gemini Live's own output carries one. Hoola's voice studio already refuses to imitate a named person and requires consent ([Hoola CLAUDE.md](/home/user/santosh-app/CLAUDE.md)), which matches this.

## Measure humanness with timing numbers and blind side-by-side votes

The old way to rate voices, asking listeners to score single sentences out of five, no longer separates good from great, because everything now scores near human. Sesame found that listeners could not tell its voice from a human on single sentences, but once they heard the previous 90 seconds of conversation they **consistently preferred the human** ([Sesame research](https://sesame.com/research)). Any test of a call voice must therefore include the conversation around it. Side-by-side votes by real users work better than fixed test sets: Stanford's Talk Arena collected 5,000 paired votes and found that 18 standard tests did not match what users actually preferred ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/blog/WillHeld/talk-arena)), and a 2026 follow-up found standard tests stop at about 0.85 agreement with user happiness ([arXiv 2605.00022](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00022v1)). When comparing many voices at once, hide which one is the human recording, because listeners otherwise punish voices that beat it ([arXiv 2411.12719](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.12719v3)).

A practical scorecard therefore mixes a few timing numbers with real votes. The timing side counts how long until the first sound (the typical case and the slow 1-in-20 case), how often the AI wrongly jumps in during her pauses, how often a "haan" or background talk wrongly stops it, how fast it goes quiet when she really interrupts, and how good its answer is after an interruption ([Full-Duplex-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04721v3)). An everyday outcome measure is how often she must repeat herself: at AI drive-throughs, 34% of customers had to repeat their order, against 22% with human staff ([QSR](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/story/the-2025-qsr-drive-thru-report/)). The vote side is a regular blind test where native speakers of each language hear two versions of the same conversation and pick the more human one. No public test covers turn-taking in Indian languages or Hinglish, so Hoola would have to run its own.

## What Hoola should do: protect the Gemini call and measure around it

Hoola is already on the right road. Its call uses the model with the widest Indian language list, at one of the lowest prices, in the same "duplex voice in front, brain behind" design the industry is moving to, and it has already fixed the two worst human-sound mistakes: the mixed voices from pre-recorded clips, and fake breathing ([Hoola CLAUDE.md](/home/user/santosh-app/CLAUDE.md)). Nothing in the research gives a reason to replace Gemini. The top-ranked rivals publish no Indian language coverage, a relay design would add delay and lose natural interruptions, and no outside study shows any other model sounds more human in Hinglish. **Changing the engine would be the biggest risk with the least proven gain.**

The first step is pure measurement, which touches nothing she hears. Hoola already saves a scorecard for every call and has a listening test ([Hoola CLAUDE.md](/home/user/santosh-app/CLAUDE.md)). It should add the numbers above to that scorecard from logs it already has: time to first sound (typical and slow cases), wrong jump-ins during her pauses, false stops caused by "haan" or a TV, time to go quiet when she cuts in, and how often she repeats herself. Only after a few weeks of these numbers is it clear what, if anything, needs fixing.

The second step is a careful check of the sound path, again without changing the model. Two things matter most: that the phone app wipes the AI's playback the instant Gemini says she interrupted, and that the microphone runs in the phone's call mode so echo is removed ([Gemini Live best practices](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/best-practices); [Pipecat](https://docs.pipecat.ai/client/concepts/choosing-a-transport)). If both already work, leave them alone.

Any change she might hear should go through one gate: built behind an off switch, tried first on a test copy of the call, judged by a blind side-by-side vote from native speakers plus the scorecard, and switched on for real users only if it clearly wins. The candidates worth that trial, from smallest risk up, are a spoken-form rule in the persona (say "baarah sau unchaas rupaye" for ₹1,249, read OTP codes digit by digit), a "do not guess the language from accent, ignore a single 'haan'" rule ([OpenAI voice prompting](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/voice-prompting)), a fair test of a few of the 30 ready-made voices in Hinglish, and a small trial of the end-of-turn silence setting inside Google's suggested 500-800 ms range ([capabilities](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/capabilities)). Opening the call to more languages, for users who want them, belongs at the end of this list and should be one language at a time, with native listeners for each, because Gemini's quality in smaller languages such as Odia or Assamese has never been measured.

Some rules hold regardless. Keep one voice on the call and never mix in clips from another engine. Do not send the removed emotion switch, and do not try to turn off proactive audio, because both break the session on 3.8 ([capabilities](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/capabilities)). Do not sprinkle "um" into confident answers; use one short action line before a tool runs instead. When Hoola's avatar speaks to anyone other than its owner, such as another business, it should say in its first line that it is an AI assistant and for whom, which meets the EU rule and India's spoken-notice rule in spirit ([Paul Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/eu-finalises-transparency-rules-for-ai-generated-content); [Khaitan & Co](https://www.khaitanco.com/thought-leadership/MeitY-notifies-the-IT-Amendment-Rules-2026)). A cloned voice stays limited to a user's own voice, checked by a live read-aloud, and stays off the live call, which in any case uses Google's ready-made voices.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the question from "which model has the most human voice" to "which call has the most human manners". Voice quality has largely been solved on single sentences; the open problems are timing, handling interruptions and noise, sounding native in each language, and switching correctly inside mixed speech like Hinglish. Those are mostly things to measure and tune around a working engine, not reasons to swap engines. "Every language of the world" is still out of reach for live calls: the honest promise today is good quality in a few dozen well-recorded languages, usable quality in perhaps a hundred, and unknown quality beyond that.

For Hoola, the biggest gap is not a missing model but missing proof. No public test yet measures Hinglish or Indian-language call manners, so Hoola's own scorecard and blind votes from native speakers, collected without touching the call, could become something rivals do not have: real evidence of what makes an Indian call feel human. Until that evidence points clearly at a change, the working Gemini call is the asset to protect.
